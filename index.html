Three suites, one Divergence wave

Six clients, played the way a real six-box party plays — a BRD/DNC dual-wielding between song volleys, a BLU/WAR and WAR/SAM on the mob, a COR/DNC shooting from melee range, a GEO/WHM and WHM/SCH keeping everyone standing — inside an 18-player alliance. Three feature-bearing GearSwap suites fight it on the same gear: Mirdain 1.7.3 as the release ships, the original include it grew from, Mirdain 1.5.12, and Selindrile. Each runs in two states — lean, with the optional machinery switched off, and moderate, at its own out-of-the-box defaults plus the two modes such a party actually plays. Bars in every chart are ranked largest-first within each category, and every chart says which direction is better. How all of it was measured, and where the numbers can mislead, is set out in full at the foot of the page.

Written by Rahvin, developer of the Mirdain 1.7 fork. Of the three engines compared, only 1.7.3 is his: Mirdain 1.5.12 is the original include as its author, Mirdain, shipped it, and Selindrile is a third author's suite, measured at its 2026-07-23 pinned upstream commit. The report is built to be checked rather than trusted: every feature claim was read from each suite's source, not its documentation; the measurement method is published in full at the foot of the page; every figure comes from the harness and is spliced in unedited; and the caveats section states where the numbers can mislead.

What each suite is — and what it does for you

A fair comparison starts by saying what each suite is trying to be, because these are not three attempts at the same product. Selindrile is a self-contained product: it aims to play the character — a turn-key automated character with an on-screen HUD, some thirty optional automation subsystems, and a packet-reaction engine, all inside one suite. The Mirdain engines draw their boundary narrower by design: they are gear-swapping engines meant to do that one job, whether the player drives the character by hand or pairs them with outside automation software that handles spell and ability sequencing. What Selindrile builds in, Mirdain deliberately leaves outside GearSwap — a scope choice on both sides, and the single most important fact for reading the moderate columns below. Within that boundary, 1.5.12 is the original include — modes, keybinds, a status box, movement gear, Treasure Hunter — and 1.7.3 is the fork's current engine, which keeps that surface and adds the enchanted-item engine, Hoxne Ampulla automation, disciplined gear reporting, lock modes for capacity-point gear, and the only cross-client received-spell gear system in the field. Its command surface has grown from 24 to 47 against 1.5.12, and it carries that while being the smaller file of the two 1.7 releases — it removes about 300 lines of engine while adding features. Every claim below was read from each suite's source, not its README.

Who wins what

Lower is better in every measured row, and every figure is drawn live from the run data below. A winner here is a fact about this scenario on this gear — the grid above is the other half of any fair reading, because the suites are not doing the same amount of work by design.

Processor cost — the whole box, both states

Whole-box cost: all six clients, weighted by how often each event fires, per second of fighting. A handful of measured paths carry no weight in this model and so sit outside these totals, which makes every column read a little low — the same way for all three, so the comparison holds even though the absolutes are conservative. Moderate is each suite's own out-of-the-box defaults plus Hoxne on the Mirdain 1.7.3 Bard and Treasure Hunter Tag on the Corsair (all three suites). Lean is every chat channel off, Hoxne off and Treasure Hunter None — with one deliberate exception, the cross-client received-spell system, which keeps its shipped default and so is running in Mirdain 1.7.3's lean column but does not exist on 1.5.12. For Selindrile lean is the parity tier. It is the column closest to a pure efficiency claim, because it is the closest the three come to doing identical work.

Selindrile's cost is what its automation delivers: its 0.5 s tick walks whole subsystem checklists and its reaction engine runs on every action packet in range, so its columns price a product, not a defect. The Mirdain columns price the gear-swap layer alone: sequencing, when a player wants it automated, comes from software outside GearSwap, which no column here can see. The Mirdain engines' own story is the generation gap: 1.7.3 against 1.5.12, release against release, in both states — and under this grid the newer engine reads cheaper in both. Every column is a tiny share of one processor core — the frame section below puts all three against the actual budget.

Processor cost per character

The party splits three ways, and the cost follows the split. The Bard, Warrior and Blue Mage melee — dual-wield rounds every 1.6 seconds, the Chango every 1.6 under Hasso and Haste Samba — with the Bard stepping out for a full five-song volley when the earliest buff nears its end and the Blue Mage casting only its self-buffs and the occasional stun. The Geomancer and White Mage cast, so theirs is action chains, with the White Mage pairing Accession and Divine Seal with AoE spells on their recasts. The Corsair shoots between its own melee rounds — a Death Penalty shot every 2.5 seconds at 65% Snapshot — and carries Treasure Hunter on Tag in the moderate state on every suite. That is not why it is expensive, though — it is the costliest client in the lean columns too, where Treasure Hunter is off; the gun's own rotation is the reason, and Treasure Hunter adds to it.

Per-client cells say where each suite spends: Selindrile's casters pay for its MiniQueue and per-action bookkeeping, and its whole roster pays the 0.5 s tick; the Mirdain engines' spread between the two states is chat and the two practical modes. The 1.7.3-over-1.5.12 gap is largest on the casters and the Corsair, whose action chains are where the engines differ most; on the Bard and Warrior melee it narrows to a fraction of a microsecond, their rotations spending the least time in engine code — but it does not reverse: the newer release is cheaper on every client in both states.

Memory churn — allocation per second, by suite

Allocation is the cleanly measurable memory number, and it separates the three suites more sharply than CPU does. The Mirdain generation gap is the direct consequence of building gear sets with merge_into, which writes into an existing table, instead of set_combine, which allocates two or three fresh tables per input set — and Selindrile's figure prices its per-action bookkeeping and reaction machinery. Chat settings barely move any column, because the garbage is gear-set tables rather than strings. The whole sandbox — suite plus the harness's own per-event tables — churns well above the engine-only figure () and moves in the same direction; that figure is not engine-attributable and is kept out of the chart.

Memory held per character

The chart is the cost of one more client, which is the figure that compares. Running several boxes on one machine, a suite can be paid for twice: once for anything it loads once and shares, and then again for every character that loads on top of that. Only the second is a per-client property, and the two behave very differently here. Whichever character loads first also pays whatever its suite loads once, so its reading is not comparable with the rest — the same character retains 2,502 KB in one Selindrile column and 866 KB in the other, differing only in which of the two got there first. The bars therefore show the marginal figure, taken from the characters that load into an already-running process.

How much is actually shared separates the suites more sharply than the bars do. A genuinely one-time load is gone by the second column, and that test splits them cleanly: Selindrile's first-loader premium falls from 1,639 KB to 3 KB, so about 1,636 KB is loaded once and shared. Mirdain's does not fall at all — 1.7.3 reads the same premium in both columns — because each character re-reads the gear library into its own environment rather than sharing one copy. Mirdain's genuinely shared cost is therefore near zero (about 84 KB on 1.5.12, and nothing measurable on 1.7.3), and what looked like a shared cost is simply the Bard being a heavier character to dress on these engines.

Which suite keeps the least depends entirely on how many boxes you run. At one character Selindrile keeps the most — about 2,500 KB against 1,571 for Mirdain 1.7.3 — because it pays its shared load up front for a single beneficiary. By three characters it has drawn level, and by six it keeps the least by a wide margin: roughly 6,800 KB against 9,400 for 1.7.3 and 8,400 for 1.5.12. The Mirdain 1.7 figure over 1.5.12 buys the whole enhanced feature set. Both Mirdain columns load a gear library of comparable size — a 1.7-line addition the measured 1.5.12 column also loads, so that neither engine is dressed in nothing — though each release carries its own copy, 3,726 entries on 1.7.3 against 3,697 on the older tree; a real 1.5.12 install, which never shipped a library at all, would retain less. Nothing in any suite forces a collection, and one costs about a millisecond on either Mirdain engine against about five on Selindrile, whose live heap is far larger.

Garbage collection — the bill for allocation, not a separate budget

Collector time is not a separate budget — it is the bill for the allocation above, so the way to reduce it is to allocate less. It is not charted here, deliberately — not because the total is negligible, but because the individual readings sit at the edge of what this harness's clock can resolve, which is why one of them lands on zero. Drawing them would imply a precision the measurement does not have.

What can be said, across every column: collector work runs µs per client per second of combat, at most of engine wall time, and one full collection of a client's live heap costs . Nothing in any suite forces a collection.

The per-cell spread swamps the differences between the two Mirdain engines, whose ranges overlap completely, so they cannot be ranked against each other on this metric. Selindrile is the one separation the resolution supports: its cells do not overlap either Mirdain engine's at any client. The clock available here resolves to 1 millisecond against a pass that costs about sixteen, which is the whole reason. Allocation is the quantity that is cleanly measurable, and it is the one to watch.

Frame time — none of the three comes close to filling a frame, and the footprints differ by design

The question a frame budget answers is not "what does the average frame cost" but "what does the worst one cost". Across the simulated minutes per state, the single most expensive frame on any client is the one where a whole cast chain lands — and even the worst cell in the whole grid stays a small share of a frame. The per-frame footprints are a design difference worth naming: Mirdain 1.5.12 registers no per-frame handler at all — its work rides packet events, and its prerender cell in the background table is the bare dispatch, priced for completeness; Mirdain 1.7.3 registers one prerender pass that gates its enchanted-item and Hoxne ticks behind clock compares; Selindrile registers two — its 0.5 s automation tick and its own 0.1 s movement poll — whose combined steady pass is the costliest of the three and is where its frame column comes from.

To place that sliver in context, the track below stacks it against estimates of what else occupies the same frame on a real client: the game's own rendering and simulation — which in a crowded Divergence pull consumes most of the budget, and is why frame rates sag there with no addons at all — and a chat-parsing addon of the Battlemod class, which rewrites every battle line and is notorious for spiking during dense combat text. Those two segments are rough comparison figures, not measurements; the suite segments are the measured worst frames. The point survives any reasonable estimate: the game takes milliseconds, a heavy chat addon takes on the order of a millisecond in a busy fight, and even the busiest suite's worst moment is a sliver.

Cost of one action — same casts, three different bills

One full chain: pretarget, precast, the outgoing chunk, the action packets, midcast, aftercast — and whatever follow-up work the suite itself queues — with every recast-table marshal and all chat formatting up to the point a line is handed to Windower. Identical gear lands on every suite, so the differences are plumbing: how much selection, merging and bookkeeping each suite runs to dress the same cast.

Read the shape, not just the ranks: the Mirdain engines layer sets and (on 1.7.3) report what they chose at each phase, while Selindrile selects one merged set but runs its full refresh-and-check pass at the head of every action, so its per-action floor is higher before any gear moves. A dash means the suite has no such action on that client's rotation bench.

Background events — where the suites' event footprints show

Background paths scale with alliance traffic rather than with how often this character acts, and they are also where the three suites genuinely register different work. A dash is a path the suite does not have, or one not benched for it: the IPC rows exist only on Mirdain 1.7.3 (1.5.12 predates the spell-received system); the update auto rebuild is a Mirdain self-command; the mode-change row is benched through Selindrile's own command machinery, so the Mirdain engines' mode commands are simply not measured there; and on a battle-log line only Selindrile does more than dispatch-floor work, because only its proc watcher registers a text handler.

The row that scales hardest with alliance size and exists on both Mirdain engines is the foreign-weaponskill handler, which runs for every entity's weaponskill in range; that is where 1.7.3's shape shows against 1.5.12's on the same path. The bystander IPC parse scales the same way but has no 1.5.12 counterpart to compare against, since that engine has no received-spell system — it is a cost 1.7.3 pays for a feature, not a saving. Costlier one-off events sit in the table rather than the chart, where their scale would flatten everything else.

Killing a mob and moving on

A party does not stand on one mob for five minutes. Each client here fights one for 30–68 seconds, kills it, disengages, runs 5–11 seconds to the next and re-engages — five or six times a run. Disengaging and re-engaging each run a full set build and equip through every suite's status-change path, and the movement rows show two designs: the Mirdain engines poll position inside their packet-driven engine pass, while Selindrile polls on its own per-frame handler. At five or six kills per five minutes none of this dominates a per-second total, but every step is real work a suite does dozens of times a night.

Received-spell gear: the race only two suites can enter

When a peer casts a tracked spell at you, gear for the incoming effect is worth wearing only if it lands before the spell does. Two of the three suites have a mechanism at all, and they are structurally different. Mirdain 1.7.3's is cross-client: the caster's client sends an IPC message at cast registration, so the target's window is the whole cast time minus one same-machine hop. Selindrile's is local: its Sel-Stahp engine reacts to the server's own casting echo, so its floor is the server round-trip — and its swap is suppressed while the receiver is mid-action, then rides a queued command hop. Mirdain 1.5.12 predates the system entirely.

The verdicts below replay both mechanisms against the same fast-cast-capped spell windows, at three stated server delays. Engine-side processing is microseconds on both sides — irrelevant against windows measured in hundreds of milliseconds; the race is decided by when each mechanism can know.

Which suite fits which player

The run in one paragraph: Mirdain 1.7.3 is the cheapest engine of the three in both states — CPU and allocation against Mirdain 1.5.12 box-wide — and it is cheaper on every client in both states, though on the Bard and the Warrior in the moderate state the margin is under two percent, which the caveats say is inside this run's noise and should not be read as a result. What it does not take is memory: 1.5.12 keeps less per additional client, and the newer engine's larger resident cost is the enhanced feature set. Selindrile's columns are the grid's largest on CPU and churn — bought behaviour, per the caveats — while it takes memory per client and default chat volume outright. No suite comes near a dropped frame, and the received-spell race is Mirdain 1.7.3's by design, Selindrile entering only locally.

A lean gear-swapper, nothing running you didn't ask for. Mirdain 1.7.3 in its lean state — every channel off, Hoxne off, Treasure Hunter None, and only the received-spell system still at its shipped default — is the efficiency floor of the three: µs/s box-wide, the lowest CPU and allocation columns of the three, with the gear library's swap HP ordering still working underneath. Mirdain 1.5.12 costs more box-wide in both states and on every client, and keeps less memory per additional client; that is the trade, and the newer engine run quiet is the cheaper lean suite.

A gear-swap layer beside outside automation software. The Mirdain engines are designed as exactly this — sequencing deliberately left outside GearSwap — and Mirdain 1.7.3 at its defaults adds the layers a driven character benefits from: set naming, empty-set detection and a pre-equip wearability check, the received-spell IPC, enchanted-item handling, and held gear for capacity-point pieces. It costs µs/s box-wide and is the loudest default chat column of the grid, each of its four reporting channels its own toggle. What the outside tool itself costs, no column here can see.

Automation living inside the GearSwap suite. That is Selindrile, plainly: some thirty subsystems on its half-second tick, an on-screen HUD, packet-reaction gear — it aims to play the character, and its columns price that ambition: the grid's highest CPU ( µs/s) and allocation ( KB/s), still a small share of one core and never close to filling a frame, alongside the lowest cost per additional character — it keeps the most of the three at one box and the least from about three onwards — and the quietest default chat. For automation without running software outside GearSwap, it is the suite built for the job.

How this was measured

Scenario
Six local clients — BRD/DNC at 60 fps; GEO/WHM, WHM/SCH, COR/DNC, BLU/WAR and WAR/SAM at 30 — inside an 18-player alliance against a 15-mob wave. 300 simulated seconds per configuration, replayed from a fixed seed so every engine sees a byte-identical event stream. Each client fights a mob for 30–68 s, kills it, disengages, runs 5–11 s to the next one and re-engages: five or six times a run. The Bard dual-wields Naegling and Centovente, keeps Haste Samba up out of its TP, and re-sings its five buff songs as one volley when the earliest buff is a minute from dropping; the Blue Mage dual-wields Naegling and Thibron and casts only self-buffs plus the occasional stun; the Corsair dual-wields Rostam ×2 in melee range while shooting Death Penalty; the Warrior swings Chango behind Utu Grip; the White Mage pairs Accession and Divine Seal with AoE spells on their recasts. Day and weather are pinned for the whole run — Fire day, single Dark weather — so every suite's day, weather and distance gear checks run and are priced on each eligible cast, always resolving down the same branch.
Cadence
Not hand-picked gaps. Every timing rule is sourced and self-tested against the wiki’s own worked examples: attack rounds from weapon delay with the three haste categories capped separately and a global 80% floor; dual wield on combined delay; TP per hit from delay-per-hand; Fast Cast capped at 80% of cast time and contributing half its value to recast; magical and equipment haste reducing recast, job-ability haste not; ranged shots from the gun’s delay at ~106 delay/second with Snapshot capped at 70% combined and Rapid Shot rolled per shot from its flat 1/16–8/16 distribution. Weapon delay comes from the game’s item data, cast times and recasts from its spell data. A reaction interval and a per-job duty factor separate what the lockout permits from what the fight asks for.
What runs
Each suite's complete shipped code, compiled into a per-client sandbox in GearSwap's own load order: each Mirdain release with the job files and gear library that release ships; Selindrile's full library chain with its bundled organizer include. Registrations, schedules and settings run as shipped. Nothing is extracted or paraphrased.
States
Lean: every chat channel off, Hoxne off, Treasure Hunter None; for Selindrile this is the parity tier — its one GearSwap-level verbosity key already defaults off. Moderate: each suite's own first-load defaults untouched (for Mirdain that is info and warn on, debug and the gear trace off), plus Hoxne ON-Allow Critical on the Bard where the engine has the mode (Mirdain 1.7.3) and Treasure Hunter Tag on the Corsair on all three — Selindrile by its library mode, the Mirdain engines by their own command. Default-on automation and buff tracking run untouched everywhere; the identical seeded buff stream (the Warrior's Aftermath cycle, roll pairs, samba daze) reaches every suite's own buff-change path.
Gear
The same six loadouts everywhere, but each Mirdain release brings its own copy of them: 1.7.3 runs the job files and gear library it ships with, and 1.5.12 runs those files as the previous release shipped them, which is the newest set it can load — it cannot load 1.7.3's, which call an engine hook it does not have. The pair therefore differs by a release, not by an engine file alone; the gear library is a 1.7-line addition in both cases, loaded under the 1.5.12 column so that both engines dress real gear. Selindrile runs generated ports of the same six loadouts — mappings judged by hand, gear transcribed by machine, and checked set-for-set against the originals. Its upstream files stay pinned byte-for-byte; the as-shipped tier is bootable but deliberately not in this grid, because its shipped example gear is dated, and measuring it would compare sample authorship rather than engines.
GearSwap layer
A port of the real set_merge / unify_slots, preserving per-call allocation shape — including its silent tolerance of nil arguments, which one live job file depends on.
Timing
Unit costs come from continuous hot loops of 250 ms or more, on the real clock, outside the sandbox. Inside it, time is virtual and driver-controlled, so busy windows, critical windows, warn throttles and item cooldowns all run on their true cadence.
Allocation
Two figures at two scopes. Engine allocation is each event type’s heap delta, measured with the collector stopped, weighted by event rate — that is the number charted. Whole-sandbox allocation is the heap delta across the entire timeline; it includes the driver’s own tables, runs well above the engine figure, and is reported only as corroboration.
Collector
Both sides timed in interleaved chunks of about 0.25 s, ten a side, the stopped side collecting between chunks so only one chunk of garbage is ever live. Interleaving also keeps run order from favouring either side.

What these numbers are not, and where they can mislead

A cell is only comparable within this run. The scenario, rotations and cadence are regenerated for each run of the suite, so a figure here is not the same measurement as the matching cell in an older copy of this report. The suites are compared against each other on identical input, which is the comparison that matters.

The two Mirdain columns are releases, not engine files. 1.7.3 is measured as it ships — its engine, its gear library and its job files together — while 1.5.12 runs the job files as the previous release shipped them, that being the newest set an engine of its age can load. So the gap between those two columns is what a player moving between releases would experience, and it is not purely the engine: some of it is the gear library and the job files moving with it. Holding those fixed instead would price fallbacks no player ever hits, because 1.7.3 re-keys the ranged weaponskill sets; there is no arrangement that isolates the engine and stays realistic, so the realistic one is reported.

One timing rule in the combat model is known to be wrong, and the Corsair carries it. When a character fires a ranged shot, this model discards the progress made toward the next melee swing and starts the timer again, where the game suspends it and resumes from where it stopped. The shooting client therefore lands fewer melee rounds here than it would in game, and every suite is charged for correspondingly less work on that character. It is the same rule for all three suites, so it does not favour any of them, but the Corsair's absolute figures are the softest on the page and will move when the rule is corrected.

Not an in-game measurement. There is no FFXI client in this environment. Windower API calls are modelled as fresh-table builds of realistic size, applied identically to every suite; chat is counted as volume, never timed, because rendering happens on Windower's side; GearSwap's own per-event overhead is excluded as identical for all three. The game-render and chat-addon segments of the frame chart are order-of-magnitude estimates for context, not measurements. An in-game load remains the final word.

The lean columns are the only pure efficiency claim. Moderate differences are bought behaviour: a suite doing more work is not slower, it is charging for a feature the others do not attempt. Read the feature grid on sheet 1 before reading any moderate rank as a verdict.

The suites draw the automation boundary in different places, and the moderate columns inherit it. Selindrile bundles play automation into the measured suite; the Mirdain engines are designed as the gear-swap layer of a setup where sequencing, if automated at all, runs outside GearSwap — invisible to this harness. A moderate-state gap between them is therefore partly a boundary difference: one bill includes automation, the other's automation is billed elsewhere or nowhere. The lean and parity columns are much less affected — optional automation is off there, with one stated exception: Mirdain's received-spell system keeps its shipped default, so 1.7.3's lean column carries a subsystem 1.5.12 does not have at all. That is why lean is the closest thing here to a pure efficiency claim rather than a perfect one.

The collector figures are an envelope, not a ranking. The clock here resolves to 1 ms and one 300-second pass costs about 16 ms, so a few-percent collector delta sits close to the floor even chunked and interleaved. The per-cell spread exceeds the differences between suites, so none can be ranked against another on this metric. Allocation, which is what drives the collector, is measured cleanly and is the number to watch.

Memory is reported per additional client, and the live-heap column is not. One lazily-loaded copy of the game's resource tables is shared across clients and charged to whichever client loads it first, so a first-loader's figure is its own cost plus its suite's shared cost — the same client reads 2,502 KB in one Selindrile column and 866 KB in the other on that basis alone. The bars therefore show the marginal per-client figure, which is steady across both states, and the shared cost is stated separately in the memory section. Two further limits on that table: the first row of every column is the character that loaded first, so it is not comparable with the five beneath it; and the live-heap column is a whole-process reading rather than a per-character one, which is why every suite's rows sit at a similar level and why the figure should be read as an envelope, never ranked. None of this is a leak in any suite. Selindrile's White Mage cancellation count in the bench notes is its own AutoCaress cycle at hot-loop cadence — understood, bounded, and never averaged into a cast figure.

Where these numbers come from, and how far to trust the small differences. Timings are wall-clock on a machine that also runs everything else, and interference can only ever make code look slower, never faster. So each measurement is timed several times and the fastest is kept, the fastest being the pass least disturbed; the columns being compared are measured next to each other in time rather than an hour apart, so that any drift is shared between them; and the slowest pass is recorded beside the fastest as a check on how quiet the machine was. Across this run's 1,702 timed cells the two agreed within 2% on 63% of them and within 10% on almost all the rest; four cells disagreed by more than 25%, and all four are among the very cheapest measurements, where the clock itself is the limit. An earlier pair of runs taken without those precautions disagreed with each other by as much as 26% on a whole column — and, more to the point, disagreed on the direction of a comparison between two Mirdain releases, one run making a release look cheaper and the other dearer. That is what these precautions exist to prevent, and it is why the guidance below is what it is. Treat gaps of a few percent as noise and read the ranks.

Some figures are quantised, and one of them is large enough to matter. The movement poll, the per-frame pass and the incoming-cast handler only do real work when an interval has elapsed or a condition is met, so measuring them over a fixed number of calls counts however many times that happened to fall inside the window — an integer, and a small one. Such a figure can land at either of two values a factor of two apart with nothing about the suite changing. This is not a small correction everywhere: the per-frame pass is multiplied by the frame count, and on the Bard at 60 fps it accounts for roughly a third of that client's whole column. Where two suites are within a few percent on a single client — the Bard and the Warrior in the moderate state — that is inside what this effect alone can move, and those cells should not be read as a result. The box-wide totals and the large gaps are unaffected.

Selindrile is measured at its pinned upstream commit of 2026-07-23 on ported gear, with its shipped defaults; claims about it are claims about that version on this scenario, nothing broader. Its measured columns exist because its library genuinely boots, casts, equips and tracks here — verified end to end before any number was recorded.

This report compares feature-bearing suites. Gear-swap frameworks that ship machinery for job authors rather than finished features for players are a different product class, and ranking one beside these three would compare unlike work; none is included here.

Chat volume, not CPU, remains the binding constraint on a six-box setup. The moderate columns emit what each suite's shipped defaults produce — most of it Mirdain 1.7.3's info channel naming every set worn, which makes it the loudest column of this grid at those defaults. The volume is mostly a setting rather than a fixed property: each of Mirdain's four reporting channels is its own toggle on both engines, and the lean column measures that state — though neither engine falls silent, since 1.5.12 prints ammo, weather and element notices regardless of all three of its toggles, and 1.7.3's command-reply channel has no toggle by design — with info and warn off, Mirdain 1.7.3 falls to lines a minute box-wide, a floor of ammo warnings and similar safety notices that deliberately ignore the toggles. The asymmetry runs the other way too: Selindrile's refusal messages and state summaries have no toggle — its lines a minute here are low because this scenario rarely refuses an action, not because a player can turn them down. Mirdain's full-channel diagnostic state (retired from this grid) emitted on the order of a thousand lines a minute box-wide; that is why the gear trace ships off by default and is documented as a diagnostic toggle.
