Three suites, one Divergence wave

Six clients fight one Divergence wave inside an 18-player alliance, played the way a real six-box party plays. A Bard dual-wields between song volleys. A Blue Mage and a Warrior stay on the mob. A Corsair shoots from melee range. A Geomancer and a White Mage keep everyone standing. Three GearSwap suites run that fight on the same gear: Rahvin GS 2.0 as it ships today, Mirdain 1.5.12, which is the original include it grew from, and Selindrile. Each suite runs in two states. Lean has the optional machinery switched off. Moderate uses each suite's own defaults plus the two modes such a party actually plays. The method and the caveats are at the foot of the page.

Written by Rahvin, the developer of Rahvin GS. The suite began as a fork of Mirdain's include and took its own name when it was split into components. Of the three engines here, only Rahvin GS is the author's own. Mirdain 1.5.12 is the original include as Mirdain shipped it. Selindrile is a third author's suite, measured at its upstream commit of 2026-07-23. Rahvin GS 2.0 is measured as it ships, at the exact commit that runs on the author's own six boxes (GearSwap 9ac6426, 2026-09-12), with its engine, gear library and job files together. Every feature claim was read from each suite's source rather than its documentation. Every charted figure comes from the measurement run unedited. The few counts quoted in the text, such as library sizes, can be checked against the same run.

What each suite is

A fair comparison starts with what each suite is trying to be, because these are not three attempts at the same product. Selindrile is a self-contained product. It aims to play the character: an automated character with an on-screen HUD, some thirty optional automation subsystems and a packet-reaction engine, all inside one suite. The two Mirdain-lineage engines draw a narrower boundary on purpose. They swap gear and do nothing else. The player either drives the character by hand or pairs them with outside software that handles spell and ability sequencing. What Selindrile builds in, this lineage leaves outside GearSwap. That choice is the single most important fact for reading the moderate columns below. Within that boundary, 1.5.12 is the original include, with modes, keybinds, a status box, movement gear and Treasure Hunter. Rahvin GS 2.0 keeps that surface and adds an enchanted-item engine, Hoxne Ampulla automation, disciplined gear reporting, a slot-ownership ladder for held gear, four on-screen display styles, per-character settings and the only cross-client received-spell gear system in the field. It is a much larger body of code: about 11,900 lines across fifteen component files, against 3,300 lines in 1.5.12's single file.

Frame time

A frame budget is about the worst frame, not the average one. Over the simulated minutes in each state, the most expensive frame on any client is the one where a whole cast chain lands. Even the worst cell in the grid stays a small share of a frame. The per-frame footprints differ by design. Mirdain 1.5.12 registers no per-frame handler at all. Its work rides packet events, and its prerender row in the background table is the bare dispatch, priced for completeness. Rahvin GS 2.0 registers two prerender handlers, kept separate so a fault in one cannot take the other down. One runs the enchanted-item and Hoxne ticks behind clock compares. The other is the received-gear failsafe, which returns at once unless a hold has outlived its cast. Selindrile registers two as well: its 0.5 s automation tick and its own 0.1 s movement poll. Their combined steady pass is the costliest of the three, and it is where Selindrile's frame column comes from.

To put that sliver in context, the track below stacks it against estimates of what else occupies the same frame on a real client. The game's own rendering and simulation take most of the budget in a crowded Divergence pull, which is why frame rates sag there with no addons at all. A chat-parsing addon of the Battlemod class rewrites every battle line and is known for spiking during dense combat text. Those two segments are rough comparison figures, not measurements. The suite segments are the measured worst frames. Whatever the estimate, the point stands. The game takes milliseconds, a heavy chat addon takes on the order of a millisecond in a busy fight, and even the busiest suite's worst moment is a sliver.

Cost of one action

One full chain is pretarget, precast, the outgoing chunk, the action packets, midcast and aftercast, plus whatever follow-up work the suite queues for itself. It includes every recast-table marshal and all chat formatting, up to the point a line is handed to Windower. Identical gear lands on every suite, so the differences are plumbing: how much selection, merging and bookkeeping each suite runs to dress the same cast.

The shapes differ. The two lineage engines layer sets, and Rahvin GS reports what it chose at each phase. Selindrile selects one merged set but runs its full refresh-and-check pass at the head of every action, so its per-action floor is higher before any gear moves.

The status-box repaint row prices that redraw on its own:  µs on Rahvin GS 2.0 against on 1.5.12. The buff-pair row is the buff-change hook every engine pays on every buff gained or lost. On a battle-log line only Selindrile does more than dispatch-floor work, because only its proc watcher registers a text handler.

Killing a mob and moving on

A party does not stand on one mob for five minutes. Each client here fights one for 30 to 68 seconds, kills it, disengages, runs 5 to 11 seconds to the next and re-engages, five or six times a run. Disengaging and re-engaging each run a full set build and equip through every suite's status-change path. The movement rows show two designs. The Mirdain-lineage engines poll position inside their packet-driven engine pass. Selindrile polls on its own per-frame handler. At five or six kills per five minutes none of this dominates a per-second total, but every step is real work a suite does dozens of times a night.

The received-spell race

When a peer casts a tracked spell at you, gear for the incoming effect is only worth wearing if it lands before the spell does. Two of the three suites have a mechanism at all, and the two are built differently. Rahvin GS 2.0's is cross-client. The caster's client sends an IPC message at cast registration, so the target's window is the whole cast time minus one same-machine hop. Selindrile's is local. Its Sel-Stahp engine reacts to the server's own casting echo, so its floor is the server round trip. Its swap is also suppressed while the receiver is mid-action, and then rides a queued command hop. Mirdain 1.5.12 predates the system entirely.

The chart replays both mechanisms against the same spell windows, capped by Fast Cast, at three stated server delays. Engine-side processing is microseconds on both sides, which is nothing against windows measured in hundreds of milliseconds. The race is decided by when each mechanism can know.

Which suite fits which player

Rahvin GS 2.0 is the cheapest engine of the three in both states. Against Mirdain 1.5.12 it uses CPU and allocation box-wide, and it is cheaper on every client in both states by a quarter or more. The narrowest margin, the Bard's in the moderate state, is well outside this run's noise. Memory is the exception. 1.5.12 keeps less per additional client, and the newer engine's larger resident cost is its feature set. Selindrile's columns are the largest on CPU and churn, which is bought behaviour, as the caveats explain. It wins memory per client and default chat volume outright. No suite comes near a dropped frame. The received-spell race is Rahvin GS 2.0's by design, and Selindrile enters it only locally.

A lean gear-swapper that runs nothing you did not ask for. Rahvin GS 2.0 in its lean state, with every channel off, Hoxne off, Treasure Hunter None and only the received-spell system at its shipped default, is the efficiency floor of the three at  µs/s box-wide. It has the lowest CPU and allocation columns, and the gear library's swap HP ordering still works underneath. Mirdain 1.5.12 costs more box-wide in both states and on every client, and keeps less memory per additional client. That is the trade.

A gear-swap layer beside outside automation software. The Mirdain-lineage engines are designed as exactly this, with sequencing left outside GearSwap on purpose. Rahvin GS 2.0 at its defaults adds the layers a driven character benefits from: set naming, empty-set detection, a pre-equip wearability check, the received-spell IPC, enchanted-item handling and held gear for capacity-point pieces. It costs  µs/s box-wide and is the loudest default chat column of the grid, though each of its four reporting channels has its own toggle. What the outside tool itself costs, no column here can see.

Automation living inside the GearSwap suite. That is Selindrile. Some thirty subsystems on its half-second tick, an on-screen HUD and packet-reaction gear. It aims to play the character, and its columns price that ambition: the grid's highest CPU ( µs/s) and allocation ( KB/s). That is still a small share of one core and never close to filling a frame. How this was measured

Scenario
Six local clients inside an 18-player alliance against a 15-mob wave. The Bard runs at 60 fps and the other five at 30. Each configuration is 300 simulated seconds, replayed from a fixed seed, so every engine sees a byte-identical event stream. Each client fights a mob for 30 to 68 s, kills it, disengages, runs 5 to 11 s to the next one and re-engages, five or six times a run. The Bard dual-wields Naegling and Centovente, keeps Haste Samba up out of its TP, and re-sings its five buff songs as one volley when the earliest buff is a minute from dropping. The Blue Mage dual-wields Naegling and Thibron and casts only self-buffs plus the occasional stun. The Corsair dual-wields two Rostams in melee range while shooting Death Penalty. The Warrior swings Chango behind an Utu Grip. The White Mage pairs Accession and Divine Seal with AoE spells on their recasts. Day and weather are pinned for the whole run, Fire day with single Dark weather, so every suite's day, weather and distance gear checks run and are priced on each eligible cast, always down the same branch.
Cadence
No hand-picked gaps. Every timing rule is sourced and self-tested against the wiki's own worked examples. Attack rounds come from weapon delay, with the three haste categories capped separately and a global 80% floor. Dual wield works on combined delay. TP per hit comes from delay per hand. Fast Cast is capped at 80% of cast time and contributes half its value to recast. Magical and equipment haste reduce recast, and job-ability haste does not. Ranged shots come from the gun's delay at about 106 delay per second, with Snapshot capped at 70% combined and Rapid Shot rolled per shot from its flat 1/16 to 8/16 distribution. Weapon delay comes from the game's item data, and cast times and recasts from its spell data. A reaction interval and a per-job duty factor separate what the lockout permits from what the fight asks for.
What runs
Each suite's complete shipped code, compiled into a per-client sandbox in GearSwap's own load order. Each of the two Mirdain-lineage trees runs with the job files and gear library it carries. Selindrile runs its full library chain with its bundled organizer include. Registrations, schedules and settings run as shipped. Nothing is extracted or paraphrased.
States
Lean is every chat channel off, Hoxne off and Treasure Hunter None. For Selindrile this is the parity tier, since its one GearSwap-level verbosity key already defaults off. Moderate is each suite's own first-load defaults, untouched. For the Mirdain-lineage engines that means info and warn on, debug and the gear trace off. On top of that, Hoxne is ON-Allow Critical on the Bard where the engine has the mode (Rahvin GS 2.0), and Treasure Hunter is Tag on the Corsair on all three, by Selindrile's library mode and by the lineage engines' own command. Default-on automation and buff tracking run untouched everywhere. The identical seeded buff stream (the Warrior's Aftermath cycle, roll pairs, samba daze) reaches every suite's own buff-change path.
Gear
The same six loadouts everywhere, but each lineage engine brings its own copy of them. Rahvin GS 2.0 runs the job files and gear library it carries. 1.5.12 runs those files as a later Mirdain-named release shipped them, the set this comparison has loaded under it since its first run. It cannot load 2.0's, which call engine hooks it does not have. So the pair differs by a whole tree, not by an engine file alone. The gear library is a later addition to the lineage in both cases, loaded under the 1.5.12 column so that both engines dress real gear. Selindrile runs generated ports of the same six loadouts, with mappings judged by hand, gear transcribed by machine and every set checked against the original. Its upstream files stay pinned byte for byte. The as-shipped tier is bootable but left out of this grid on purpose, because its shipped example gear is dated, and measuring it would compare sample authorship rather than engines.
GearSwap layer
A port of the real set_merge and unify_slots, preserving the allocation shape of each call, including its silent tolerance of nil arguments, which one live job file depends on. GearSwap's own dispatch to each suite's buff_change hook, a full set build and equip on every buff event on all three engines, fires for every column alike.
Windower crossings
The game's own API is modelled, not present. Item, mob and recast queries build tables of realistic shape per call, identically for every suite. Two kinds of crossing are counted by the sandbox and priced by a measurement taken in the game itself: a text write into an on-screen box (3.2 µs for a status-box-sized string) and a write to a screen primitive (0.18 µs, and Rahvin GS 2.0's default display draws 23 of them). Each column carries those at the rate its own timeline produced them. A chat line's call costs under half a microsecond and is left uncounted. What a chat line really costs is its rendering, which no figure here sees. Frame rate with 23 primitives showing measured the same as with none, at the game's 60 fps cap.
Timing
Unit costs come from continuous hot loops of 250 ms or more, on the real clock, outside the sandbox. Inside it, time is virtual and driver-controlled, so busy windows, critical windows, warn throttles and item cooldowns all run on their true cadence.
Allocation
Two figures at two scopes. Engine allocation is each event type's heap delta, measured with the collector stopped and weighted by event rate. That is the number charted. Whole-sandbox allocation is the heap delta across the entire timeline. It includes the driver's own tables, runs well above the engine figure, and is reported only as corroboration.
Collector
Both sides are timed in interleaved chunks of about 0.25 s, ten a side. The stopped side collects between chunks, so only one chunk of garbage is ever live. Interleaving also keeps run order from favouring either side.

Where these numbers can mislead

A cell is only comparable within this run. The scenario, rotations and cadence are regenerated for each run, so a figure here is not the same measurement as the matching cell in an older copy of this report. The suites are compared against each other on identical input.

The two lineage columns are whole trees of different ages, not engine files. Rahvin GS 2.0 is measured as it ships, with engine, gear library and job files together, at the commit running on the author's own boxes. 1.5.12 runs the job files as a later Mirdain-named release shipped them, the set this comparison has loaded under it since its first run. So the gap between those two columns is what a player moving from the original include to this suite would see, and it is not purely the engine. Some of it is the gear library and the job files moving with it, and on memory that difference dominates. Holding those fixed would price fallbacks no player ever hits, because the newer tree re-keys the ranged weaponskill sets. No arrangement isolates the engine and stays realistic, so the realistic one is reported.

One timing rule in the combat model is known to be wrong. The Corsair carries it. When a character fires a ranged shot, this model discards the progress made toward the next melee swing and starts the timer again. The game suspends the timer and resumes it. So the shooting client lands fewer melee rounds here than it would in game, and every suite is charged for a little less work on that character. The rule is the same for all three suites, so it favours none of them, but the Corsair's absolute figures are the softest on the page and will move when the rule is corrected.

Nothing here was measured in the game, except the display crossings. There is no FFXI client in this environment. Windower API calls are modelled as fresh-table builds of realistic size, applied identically to every suite, and GearSwap's own per-event overhead is excluded as identical for all three. The exception is the price of a crossing into Windower's own display code. Text-box writes and screen-primitive writes were timed in the game itself, and every column carries them at the rate its timeline produced them. Under this fight's defaults that rate is zero. The default display only redraws on a command or a change of held gear, and nothing in the modelled fight does either. Chat is counted as volume only. Its call costs under half a microsecond a line in the game, and what a chat line really costs is its rendering, which no figure here sees. The game-render and chat-addon segments of the frame chart are order-of-magnitude estimates for context, not measurements. An in-game load remains the final word.

The Mirdain-lineage columns are not comparable to any earlier copy of this report. The reason is the model, not the engines. Three corrections stand behind this page. GearSwap dispatches every buff gained or lost to the engine's own buff-change hook, which is a full set build and equip when the character is not busy. Until this run the simulation fired that hook for Selindrile alone. Both lineage engines now pay it at the rate the timeline produces buff changes, which raises their columns and makes the comparison to Selindrile like for like on that path. The allocation figures now carry that same hook too. The CPU model always priced it, and the allocation model never had, so every allocation column on every suite is higher than in earlier copies and the two models finally count the same events. The mode-change row also used to measure a real mode cycle on 1.5.12 but a missed command on the newer engine, whose command grammar the harness was not speaking. It now cycles the mode on all three. Everything on this page is one run on one model.

The lean columns are the only pure efficiency claim. Moderate differences are bought behaviour. A suite doing more work is not slower. It is charging for a feature the others do not attempt. Read the feature chart on sheet 1 before reading any moderate rank as a verdict.

The suites draw the automation boundary in different places. The moderate columns inherit that. Selindrile bundles play automation into the measured suite. The Mirdain-lineage engines are designed as the gear-swap layer of a setup where sequencing, if automated at all, runs outside GearSwap, invisible to this harness. A moderate-state gap between them is therefore partly a boundary difference. One bill includes automation, and the other's automation is billed elsewhere or nowhere. The lean and parity columns are much less affected, because optional automation is off there, with one stated exception. Rahvin GS's received-spell system keeps its shipped default, so its lean column carries a subsystem 1.5.12 does not have at all. That is why lean is the closest thing here to a pure efficiency claim rather than a perfect one.

The collector figures are an envelope, not a ranking. The clock here resolves to 1 ms and one 300-second pass costs about 16 ms, so a collector delta of a few percent sits close to the floor even when chunked and interleaved. The spread between cells exceeds the differences between suites, so none can be ranked against another on this metric. Allocation drives the collector, is measured cleanly, and is the number to watch.

Memory is reported per additional client. The live-heap column is not. One lazily loaded copy of the game's resource tables is shared across clients and charged to whichever client loads it first. A first loader's figure is therefore its own cost plus its suite's shared cost. The same client reads  KB in one Selindrile column and  KB in the other on that basis alone. The line chart builds each suite from its first booter plus the per-client figure, which is steady across both states, and the shared cost is stated separately in the memory section. Two further limits apply to the numbers table. The first row of every column is the character that loaded first, so it is not comparable with the five beneath it. And the live-heap column is a whole-process reading rather than a per-character one, which is why every suite's rows sit at a similar level. Read it as an envelope and never rank it. None of this is a leak in any suite. Every memory figure on this page is a within-run comparison and does not survive a re-ordering. All three suites are measured in one process, so what a character finds already loaded depends on what ran before it. The same character read about 190 KB higher under an earlier run that visited the columns in a different order, and the shared-cost split between the two lineage columns in particular is partly an artefact of 1.5.12 having been measured first. The per-character ranking and the several-fold gaps hold. The exact kilobytes are properties of this run's order, not of the suites. Selindrile's White Mage cancellation count in the bench notes is its own AutoCaress cycle at hot-loop cadence, understood, bounded, and never averaged into a cast figure.

How far to trust the small differences. Timings are wall-clock on a machine that also runs everything else, and interference can only make code look slower, never faster. So each measurement is timed several times and the fastest pass is kept, since the fastest is the one least disturbed. The columns being compared are measured next to each other in time rather than an hour apart, so any drift is shared between them. The slowest pass is recorded beside the fastest as a check on how quiet the machine was. Across this run's 1,430 timed cells the two agreed within 2% on 34% of them and within 10% on 97%. No cell disagreed by more than 28%, and the widest gap was 27.5% on a single Rahvin GS 2.0 foreign-melee row, one of the cheapest measurements here, where the clock itself is the limit. An earlier pair of runs taken without those precautions disagreed with each other by as much as 26% on a whole column. Worse, they disagreed on the direction of a comparison between two engines of one lineage, with one run making a release look cheaper and the other dearer. That is what these precautions exist to prevent. Treat gaps of a few percent as noise and read the ranks.

One quantised figure is large enough to matter. The movement poll, the per-frame pass and the incoming-cast handler only do real work when an interval has elapsed or a condition is met. Measuring them over a fixed number of calls counts however many times that happened inside the window, which is a small integer. Such a figure can land at either of two values a factor of two apart with nothing about the suite changing. This is not a small correction everywhere. The per-frame pass is multiplied by the frame count, and on the Bard at 60 fps it accounts for between a tenth and a little over a quarter of that client's whole column, depending on the suite. Where two suites come within a few percent on a single client, that is inside what this effect alone can move and should not be read as a result. No pair of suites is that close on any client in this run, so it moves no ranking here. The box-wide totals and the large gaps are unaffected.

Selindrile is measured at its pinned upstream commit of 2026-07-23, on ported gear, with its shipped defaults. Claims about it are claims about that version on this scenario and nothing broader. Its measured columns exist because its library genuinely boots, casts, equips and tracks here, verified end to end before any number was recorded.

This report compares feature-bearing suites. Gear-swap frameworks that ship machinery for job authors rather than finished features for players are a different product class. Ranking one beside these three would compare unlike work, so none is included.

Chat volume is still what limits a six-box setup, not CPU. The moderate columns emit what each suite's shipped defaults produce. Most of it is Rahvin GS 2.0's info channel naming every set worn, which makes it the loudest column of this grid at those defaults. The volume is mostly a setting rather than a fixed property. Neither engine falls silent. 1.5.12 prints ammo, weather and element notices regardless of its toggles, and Rahvin GS's command-reply channel has no toggle by design. With info and warn off, Rahvin GS 2.0 falls to lines a minute box-wide. That floor is one line the White Mage's own job file prints on every cast, the same on both engines, and it is not the engine's. The asymmetry runs the other way too. Selindrile's refusal messages and state summaries have no toggle. Its lines a minute here are low because this scenario rarely refuses an action, not because a player can turn them down.
